# Interior Design Website Service --- Messaging & Copy Research

## Core Positioning

### Central problem

**Their work is better than the way they're presenting it online.**

The designer may already have strong projects, professional photography,
happy clients, referrals, years of experience, Instagram attention, and
industry relationships. But when somebody hears their name and looks
them up, those assets may be scattered across Instagram, WhatsApp, PDFs,
Google, directories, and an outdated website.

The website's job is not to magically manufacture demand. Its job is to
give existing attention somewhere useful to go.

### Core proposition

> **When someone hears your name, what do they find?**
>
> A referral. An Instagram post. A conversation at an event. A Google
> search.
>
> However they discover your studio, eventually they may look you up.
>
> **We build the place they go next.**

### Strong alternative

> **Your work is better than your online presence.**

### Supporting idea

> Your best projects shouldn't disappear into an Instagram feed.
>
> Your experience shouldn't live only in conversations.
>
> Your referrals shouldn't have to explain who you are.
>
> And your next client shouldn't have to piece your studio together from
> five different places.
>
> **Give your work one place to speak for itself.**

# Real-Life Scenario Bank

## Discovery and first impression

### 1. The referred prospect Googles them

A previous client recommends the designer to a wealthy friend. The
friend Googles the studio, sees a dated site, and finds that Instagram
looks substantially better. The designer thinks, "They're going to judge
us from this before they ever speak to us."

### 2. The referral partner asks for the website

An architect, contractor, real-estate agent, or previous client says,
"Send me your website --- I'll pass it on." The designer hesitates
because they wish they had something better to send.

### 3. A luxury client compares three designers

A homeowner opens three studios side by side. One has beautiful project
pages, clear positioning, and an obvious enquiry path. The designer's
site is weaker. They think, "Our work is at least as good as theirs."

### 4. A prospect wonders whether the studio is established

A high-value prospect finds only a few old projects and little useful
studio information. The designer thinks, "We've been doing this for
eight years. Why does our website make us look new?"

### 5. Someone searches their name after meeting them

They meet someone at an event and the person says they'll look them up.
Later, the designer searches their own name and realizes the online
story is scattered.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Portfolio-sharing problems

### 6. The Instagram portfolio shuffle

A prospect asks, "Can you send me some of your recent residential work?"
The designer searches Instagram to assemble relevant posts.

### 7. The best project is buried

A project they are proud of is dozens of posts down the feed. A prospect
may never find it.

### 8. The PDF portfolio problem

The designer sends a large PDF over WhatsApp or email. The prospect has
to download it rather than naturally explore the studio.

### 9. The wrong-project portfolio

A prospect wants a luxury apartment but receives a general Instagram
feed containing offices, restaurants, small homes, and unrelated work.

### 10. The "send me everything" problem

A serious prospect asks for a comprehensive portfolio. The designer
manually collects images, PDFs, folders, and links.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Expertise isn't visible

### 11. Beautiful room, no story

The prospect sees beautiful photographs but no explanation of the brief,
constraints, decisions, or scope.

### 12. "What exactly did you do?"

The prospect sees the finished interior and still asks whether the
studio handled the whole project or only the interiors.

### 13. A broad portfolio looks unfocused

Residential, commercial, offices, restaurants, villas, and bedrooms
appear without a clear structure.

### 14. Their style isn't obvious

The designer knows there is a distinct philosophy behind the work, but
the website presents isolated rooms instead of a coherent point of view.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Premium positioning

### 15. Expensive project / cheap website mismatch

The studio finishes an expensive residence and invests in excellent
photography. The website presents it inside an old layout. The designer
thinks, "We spent so much making this project look incredible, and then
we're displaying it like this."

### 16. Premium-client comparison

A high-budget homeowner compares five studios. Four have polished sites;
theirs looks noticeably less refined.

### 17. The designer avoids sending the website

A prospect asks for work. The designer instinctively sends Instagram
because they don't like sending the website.

### 18. The website makes them look cheaper than they charge

The prospect sees a dated site and then asks about fees. The designer
worries the site has already influenced the prospect's price
expectations.

## Repetitive questions

### 19. "What areas do you serve?"

Another Instagram DM asks where the studio works. The designer types the
same answer again.

### 20. "Do you do full-home interiors?"

The prospect cannot determine scope and asks over WhatsApp.

### 21. "What does your process look like?"

A serious prospect asks what happens after the first consultation.

### 22. "What's the minimum project size?"

The prospect doesn't know whether they are suitable.

### 23. "Do you handle procurement?"

The studio offers full-service work but the website does not communicate
it clearly.

### 24. An Instagram post gets strong engagement but no obvious next step

### 25. The visitor reaches the bottom and doesn't know what to do

### 26. Contact information is buried

The prospect decides they want to talk but has to hunt for an email

It asks only Name / Email / Message, leaving the prospect unsure what to

### 28. The enquiry form asks too much

A genuinely interested prospect encounters a huge form and abandons it.

# ROCK BOTTOM

This is not cartoonish disaster. It is compounding friction.

### Month 1

The website is outdated.

> "We'll redo it when things slow down."

Things don't slow down.

### Month 3

A referral comes in. The prospect gets the website link. The designer
never hears back and doesn't know why.

### Month 5

A competitor launches a beautiful website.

The designer looks at it, then their own.

> "Fuck. This looks good."

Then goes back to work.

### Month 7

The studio completes one of its best projects. They spend heavily on
photography. Five images go on Instagram. The website still has no
proper project page.

### Month 10

The designer answers the same questions again:

> "Do you work outside London?"

> "What's your process?"

> "Do you handle procurement?"

> "What sort of projects do you take?"

### Year 1

The business still functions. Referrals and Instagram still work. That
is why nothing changes.

### Year 2

The actual business is substantially better than the website.

**Offline:** established studio, strong portfolio, better clients,
experienced team.

**Online:** old website, scattered portfolio, Instagram, PDFs, WhatsApp.

### Year 2.5

A newer studio with less experience launches a much better website. Its
positioning, projects, process, proof, and enquiry path are immediately
understandable.

The established designer's site isn't.

The newer studio looks easier to understand and easier to choose.

### Year 3

The designer compensates manually:

-   better PDFs
-   Instagram Highlights
-   Google Drive folders
-   individual project links
-   longer WhatsApp explanations
-   instructions to staff about which projects to send

They are effectively building a website one prospect at a time.

The worst part is that they never know exactly what they lost.

# 14. Concrete Marketing Claims

## Make your business look more professional online

-   When someone Googles your name after meeting you, they find your
    studio---not scattered social profiles.
-   Send one URL instead of five Instagram posts, a PDF, and a WhatsApp
    explanation.
-   Your newest projects don't have to live exclusively inside an
    Instagram feed.
-   Someone can understand what you design, where you work, and how to
    enquire without messaging you first.
-   Your website tells the same story as the studio your clients
    actually experience.

## Build credibility with potential clients

-   A prospect can see projects relevant to their own before they speak
    to you.
-   Your best work isn't buried 80 posts down an Instagram feed.
-   A referred client can research you at 11 PM and find the same story
    you'd tell them in person.
-   Testimonials sit beside the work instead of being buried in WhatsApp
    screenshots.
-   Your experience is presented in one place.

## Get more high-quality leads

Do not promise lead volume. Use:

-   Give prospects enough information to decide whether your studio is
    right for their project before they enquire.
-   Let someone tell you their project, location, and scope before the
    first conversation.
-   Replace "How much for a 3BHK?" with enquiries that actually describe
    the project.
-   Show the kind of work you want more of.
-   Make the website answer the first five questions prospects usually
    ask on WhatsApp.

## Charge premium prices

Do not claim the website itself creates higher prices.

Use:

-   Before a prospect asks your fee, they can see the scale of work,
    scope of service, and projects behind it.
-   Show the process behind the finished room---not just the finished
    room.
-   Make your actual scope clear.
-   Give high-value prospects context for what they are paying for.
-   Don't let a £300,000 project be presented inside a website that
    looks built for a £300 consultation.

## Stand out from competitors

-   Give someone a reason to remember your studio beyond the
    photographs.
-   Tell visitors what kind of projects you specialize in.
-   Show the thinking behind the rooms.
-   Make your approach visible.
-   When a prospect opens five designers in five tabs, make sure yours
    doesn't look interchangeable.

## Turn website visitors into enquiries

-   When someone finishes looking through your work and wants to talk,
    give them somewhere obvious to go.
-   Put the enquiry path beside the work that created the interest.
-   Let prospects introduce their project rather than write a blank
    message.
-   Don't make someone search your footer for an email address.
-   Turn "I like their work" into "I'd like to discuss my project."

## Showcase your portfolio properly

-   Turn a finished project into something a prospect can actually
    explore.
-   Give every major project its own place.
-   Show the brief, decisions, and scope behind the photographs.
-   Put strongest projects first.
-   Send one link instead of assembling five Instagram posts.
-   A project finished three years ago can still sell the next project
    if people can find it.

## Make potential clients trust you before the first call

Reframe this as reducing uncertainty:

-   Let prospects see relevant completed projects before booking.
-   Show who is behind the studio, what you handle, and what working
    with you looks like.
-   Put client feedback beside the relevant work.
-   Answer the questions a serious homeowner has before enquiring.
-   Start the first call with their project---not a 15-minute studio
    explanation.

## Stop relying entirely on Instagram and referrals

Do not attack Instagram.

-   Keep Instagram for discovery. Give interested people somewhere to go
    deeper.
-   Give referrals one link they can forward.
-   Stop assembling portfolios manually.
-   Let Google visitors, Instagram visitors, and referrals arrive at the
    same complete picture.
-   Build a home for the work that isn't dependent on the current
    position of a social feed.

## Make the business look as good online as the work does in real life

-   Your £300k project shouldn't be presented inside a website that
    looks ten years old.
-   Professional photography should look considered on the website.
-   Moving from Instagram to the website shouldn't feel like a quality
    drop.
-   Your newest project shouldn't be showcased in a template that
    represents your old studio.
-   The website should look like the studio that exists today.
-   There shouldn't be a visible gap between the website and the actual
    work.

# 15. Objection Psychology

The deeper insight:

> **"I don't need a website" is often a rationalization for avoiding a
> purchase that feels uncertain, annoying, expensive, and non-urgent.**

But Instagram and referrals genuinely can work. The landing page should
not attack them.

## "Instagram is enough."

**Public:** "Most people find us through Instagram anyway."

**Actually thinking:** "Instagram already shows my work. Why spend
thousands?"

**Fear:** Spending money and seeing no meaningful difference.

**Tried:** Instagram, Highlights, WhatsApp, PDF, link-in-bio.

**Why it didn't fully solve it:** It can show work but doesn't
necessarily organize studio → specialization → projects → approach →
services → proof → enquiry.

**Evidence:** Instagram → Website → Relevant project → How we work →
Enquiry.

**Landing-page message:**

> **Instagram gets people interested. Your website gives them somewhere
> to decide.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## "Most of my clients come through referrals."

**Public:** "We don't really need marketing."

**Actually thinking:** "My existing network already trusts me."

**Fear:** Paying for a solution to a problem they don't have.

**Tried:** Past clients, architects, contractors, friends, property
contacts.

**Why it doesn't fully solve it:** The referral creates the name; the
prospect can still Google the name afterward.

**Evidence:**

> "You should speak to Sarah. She's fantastic."

→ Google → Website → Projects → Testimonials → Process → Enquiry.

**Landing-page message:**

> **Your referral doesn't end when someone gives your name.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## "I already have a website."

**Public:** "We already have one."

**Actually thinking:** "It's not amazing, but it works."

**Fear:** Spending money replacing something functional.

**Tried:** Freelancer, template, friend, DIY, agency.

**Why it may no longer solve it:** Business changed, website didn't.

**Evidence:** Then vs Now.

**Landing-page message:**

> **The question isn't whether you have a website. It's whether it still

# 17. Visual Direction

The intended feeling:

**Architecture publication × independent design studio × portfolio**

## Use

-   warm off-white background
-   charcoal/black typography
-   one restrained accent color
-   huge editorial headlines
-   thin rules
-   tiny uppercase metadata
-   asymmetric grid
-   images breaking the grid
-   sparse handwritten annotations
-   deliberate whitespace
-   oversized section numbers
-   editorial captions

## Avoid

-   SaaS gradients
-   rounded-card overload
-   generic agency layouts
-   stock photos
-   fake testimonials
-   fake client logos
-   fake statistics
-   "10x your leads"
-   excessive animation
-   huge navigation
-   15 service cards

# 19. Final Strategic Principle

The research should not make the landing page enormous.

Use the research as source material.

The landing page needs to create:

**Recognition → Desire → Proof → Objection removal → Process → Action**

The most important recurring image is:

> **Someone has just heard your name. They pull out their phone and
> Google you. What do they see?**

That single moment connects:

-   discovery
-   referrals
-   Instagram
-   Google
-   reputation
-   portfolio
-   competition
-   perceived value
-   pricing
-   enquiry

The offer is not:

> "We make beautiful websites."

It is:

> **When someone who has just discovered or been referred to your studio
> looks you up, give them one place that shows them why they should take
> the next step.**
